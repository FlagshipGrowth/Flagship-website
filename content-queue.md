### Topic: Repeat purchase / Subscribe & Save / pack size laddering
Suggested slug: amazon-repeat-purchase-strategy
Suggested title direction: "Your Best Amazon Customer Is Already in Your Account"

Source material:

Your best customer is already in your account and you are ignoring them.

Amazon brands are obsessed with new-to-brand and almost blind to the second purchase. That is backwards, especially going into Q4.

Here is the part most sellers miss. A repeat buyer costs you nothing in ad spend, converts without comparison shopping, and improves the sales velocity that drives your organic rank. One reorder is worth more than one new sale, and it is cheaper.

Three levers actually move it, and none of them are complicated.

Subscribe and Save, if you sell anything consumable. Most brands enable it and never merchandise it. Say the reorder cadence in the images and the A+, not just in the buy box.

Pack size laddering. Give the proven buyer a bigger, better-value option. A single-unit buyer who returns for a three-pack has doubled their lifetime value in one click.

The insert and the follow-up. Not a review beg. A reason and a moment to come back.

We worked with a pet brand where 89% of revenue came from first-time buyers. That is a treadmill, and their ACoS reflected it.

We built a three-pack, pushed Subscribe and Save into the creative, and rewrote the insert around when to reorder rather than asking for stars.

Nine months later repeat purchases were 34% of revenue and blended ACoS had dropped six points, because a third of their sales no longer needed an ad to happen.

Growth does not always mean more traffic. Sometimes it means the same people, twice.

What percentage of your revenue came from repeat buyers last quarter? Most sellers cannot answer that.

### Topic: Q4 ad pacing — October/November/December budget sequencing
Suggested slug: amazon-q4-ad-pacing-strategy
Suggested title direction: "October Is the Cheapest Ad Month of Q4 — Most Brands Sit It Out"

Source material:

October is the cheapest ad month of Q4 and most brands sit it out.

They save the budget for November, walk into the most expensive CPC environment of the year cold, and call it strategy.

Here is why that is expensive.

Ad costs climb through Q4 as every seller with a credit card piles in. In October, CPCs are still close to normal. By late November you are paying a premium for the same click.

But cost is only half of it. The bigger issue is that ranking takes time. A keyword you start bidding on in November does not have the sales history to rank organically during the weeks that matter. You are renting position at peak prices instead of owning it.

So we pace the opposite way.

October is for buying rank cheaply on the keywords you intend to win in December, and for testing creative while the mistakes are still affordable.

November is for defending the positions October earned and going hard on the terms that already convert for you.

December is for margin. Pull back on discovery, ride the branded and high-intent traffic, and stop paying tuition in the last three weeks.

A brand we work with had always front-loaded November. Last year we moved 30% of their Q4 budget into October.

Their November CPC was 22% lower than the prior year for the same keywords, because they were bidding from an organic position instead of buying one. Q4 revenue was up 41% on a 12% higher spend.

Q4 is not a spending contest. It is a sequencing problem, and it starts before anyone feels festive.

Is your Q4 budget weighted toward the cheap month or the expensive one?

### Topic: Reporting — ACoS vs TACoS, what a report should lead with
Suggested slug: amazon-acos-vs-tacos-reporting
Suggested title direction: "If Your Amazon Report Leads With ACoS, You're Being Managed to the Wrong Number"

Source material:

If your agency's monthly report leads with ACoS, you are being managed to the wrong number.

I say this as someone who runs an agency. The report is where the honesty either shows up or hides.

ACoS is a useful diagnostic and a terrible headline. It goes down when you stop advertising. It goes down when you cut discovery. It goes down right before your organic rank starts sliding.

You can win ACoS for three months and be a smaller brand at the end of it.

Here is what we put at the top of a report instead.

Total revenue and contribution margin, because that is the business.

TACoS, total ad spend against total revenue, because it shows whether ads are building organic strength or replacing it.

New-to-brand orders and repeat rate, because that is future revenue.

Organic rank on the keywords that matter, because that is the asset you are building.

Then ACoS, in context, as one lever among several.

A brand came to us from another agency with a beautiful trend line. ACoS had gone from 29% to 19% over five months. The previous agency called it a win.

Total revenue was down 22% in the same window and their top three keywords had fallen off page one. They had been optimized into decline.

A report should be able to deliver bad news. If every month looks like progress, the metrics were chosen to make sure of it.

Does your reporting tell you the truth, or tell you a story?

### Topic: Launches — the "honeymoon period," narrow-launch strategy
Suggested slug: amazon-product-launch-honeymoon-period
Suggested title direction: "The Amazon Launch Honeymoon Period Is Real — Most Brands Waste It"

Source material:

The honeymoon period is real, and most brands waste it.

Amazon gives a new listing a window of extra visibility to figure out whether it deserves traffic. Get it right and you compound for a year. Get it wrong and you spend the next six months buying your way out.

Here is the sequence that works, in order.

Do not launch until the listing is finished. Not "good enough for now." Every day of the window spent on a weak main image is a day you cannot get back.

Seed reviews before you push traffic. Vine on the new ASIN, 20 to 30 reviews, because conversion with zero reviews is a hole no bid solves.

Launch narrow. One primary keyword, exact match, aggressive bid. You are not trying to be everywhere. You are trying to prove you convert on one term so the algorithm trusts you on the rest.

Price for velocity first, margin second, for the first three to four weeks only. Rank is the asset you are buying.

Then widen. Once the primary term ranks, add the adjacent cluster and let the auto campaign find what you missed.

A brand we launched last spring wanted to go live across 40 keywords on day one, because the product was good and they were impatient.

We held them to two terms for 19 days. Both hit page one. Then we opened it up.

Ninety days in, that SKU was doing $71K/month with a 14% TACoS. Their previous launch, done wide and fast, took nine months to reach half that.

Launches are not won by spending broadly. They are won by proving one thing quickly.

What is the single keyword your next launch has to own?

### Topic: BFCM deadlines checklist
Suggested slug: amazon-black-friday-cyber-monday-deadlines
Suggested title direction: "The Black Friday Deadlines Most Amazon Sellers Find Out About Too Late"

Source material:

If you have not locked your Black Friday plan, you are already behind, and not for the reason you think.

It is not the creative or the bids. It is the deadlines that have already quietly passed or are about to.

Here is the calendar most sellers find out about too late.

Deal submissions for Black Friday and Cyber Monday close months in advance. If you are not already in, you are running coupons and Best Deals instead, which is fine, but it is a different plan and you need to know that now.

FBA inbound cutoffs for holiday inventory are earlier than the shipping deadlines you are picturing, and receiving times stretch when every seller in the country ships at once.

Coupon and promotion approvals take days, not minutes, in November. Build them in October.

Creative and A+ updates need to be live before the traffic arrives, not during it. You cannot test a main image during peak week.

And the one nobody plans for: support capacity. A 4x traffic week means 4x the messages, the A-to-z claims, and the case work, at the exact moment your account health can least afford a lapse.

We run a single dated checklist for every client from October 1, with owners on each line. It is not clever. It is just early.

The brands who have a bad Q4 almost never have a bad strategy. They have a good strategy that arrived two weeks late.

What is the next Q4 deadline on your calendar, and do you know the date without looking?

### Topic: Amazon DSP — when it's worth it, when it's a tax
Suggested slug: amazon-dsp-when-its-worth-it
Suggested title direction: "Amazon DSP Is the Most Oversold Product in This Industry"

Source material:

Amazon DSP is the most oversold product in this industry.

Agencies love it because it is expensive and it sounds advanced. Most brands do not need it yet.

Here is when it is genuinely worth it, and when it is a tax.

DSP earns its keep when you have already saturated Sponsored Products on your core keywords, when you have enough traffic for retargeting audiences to be large enough to matter, and when you have a real reason to reach people off the search page. Usually that is a considered purchase, a strong repeat cycle, or a brand-building goal with budget behind it.

It is a waste when your Sponsored Products account still has obvious inefficiency in it, when your listing does not convert, or when your monthly spend is small enough that the minimums and management fees eat the upside before it appears.

I have told brands doing $150K/month to stay away from DSP and fix their listing conversion instead. That advice made us less money and made them more.

Where we do see it work: a brand with a 60-day repeat cycle used DSP to retarget past purchasers in the window before their reorder. Not prospecting. Not awareness. Just reaching people who already bought at the moment they were about to run out.

That campaign ran at a 6.1 ROAS while their prospecting DSP sat at 1.4.

Same tool. One job it is great at, one job it is mediocre at.

The rule I would give any founder: never buy a more advanced ad product to solve a problem a cheaper one is still failing at.

Is your Sponsored Products account clean enough to justify anything fancier?

### Topic: Founder story — BB-Bands / Team USA Baseball stockout lesson
Suggested slug: what-a-stockout-taught-me-about-amazon
Suggested title direction: "I Learned More From One Product Going Viral Than From Any Campaign I've Run"

Source material (first-person, Joey's own story — keep this one personal, not "we"):

I learned more from one product going viral than from any campaign I have ever run.

Before the agency, I built and ran my own Amazon brand. One of those products, BB-Bands, ended up being used by Team USA Baseball.

That should have been the win. What it actually was, was the hardest lesson I have had in this business.

Demand showed up faster than I could supply it. I had the traffic, the credibility, and the kind of proof most brands never get.

And I stocked out.

Rank slid while I waited on inventory. Competitors picked up the keywords I had earned. When stock landed, I was not buying back attention. I was buying back position, which is far more expensive.

Two things stuck with me permanently.

The first is that operations are marketing. The best campaign in the world is a liability if the supply chain behind it cannot keep a promise. I now care about a client's lead times as much as their ACoS, and clients are always surprised by that.

The second is that being the owner changes what you notice. I felt every stockout in my own bank account. Later, agency-side, I watched people manage other brands' accounts without ever having felt that.

That gap is most of why I built Flagship the way I did.

You cannot fake having lost your own money. It changes which risks you flag and how early you flag them.

The viral moment is not the achievement. Being ready for it is.

What would break in your business tomorrow if demand tripled?

### Topic: Retention philosophy — why client retention is the real strategy
Suggested slug: why-amazon-agency-retention-matters
Suggested title direction: "The Metric I Care About Most Isn't One We Report to Clients"

Source material (first-person, Joey's own POV — keep this one personal, not "we"):

The metric I care most about is not one we report to clients.

It is how long they stay.

Building an agency taught me something uncomfortable. Getting clients is a sales problem, and sales problems are solvable with effort. Keeping them is a truth problem.

Clients do not leave because of one bad month. Every account has bad months. Amazon changes something, a competitor gets aggressive, a supplier misses.

They leave because of the gap between what they were told and what they experienced.

So we run on a few rules that cost us money and keep clients.

We say no to brands we cannot help. A prospect whose problem is their product, their margin, or their supply chain does not need our PPC. Taking that retainer is stealing with extra steps.

We tell them bad news first. If something we tried did not work, that is the top of the call, not a footnote in the deck.

We cap accounts per strategist. It limits how fast we can grow. That is the point.

We report on the business, not just the channel, even when the channel number is the flattering one.

None of that is a growth hack. It is slower than the alternative.

But the brands who have been with us since the beginning are the reason we get referred, and referrals are the only lead source that compounds.

Retention is not a customer service function. It is the whole strategy, wearing a boring name.

What is your retention actually telling you about your promises?
